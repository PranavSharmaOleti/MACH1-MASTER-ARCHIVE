Section 6

AI & Connected Layer

What the digital layer must do, why it matters, and what the research actually says about each component.

Why This Exists — The Failure Baseline

The case for building a serious connected and AI layer into this vehicle is not theoretical. The largest EV scooter deployment in India to date — Ola Electric — provides the clearest available data point on what happens when software, sensor, and fleet-management functions are treated as afterthoughts.

The Ola Electric Benchmark
Mint reported that Ola Electric was receiving over 80,000 customer complaints per month before regulatory intervention.[1] HSBC analysts who visited Ola service centers in late 2024 found them "overwhelmed," and specifically attributed complaints to "sensor and software problems in the hub motor" and "battery discharge during non-usage." The Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) recorded 10,644 complaints filed between September 2023 and August 2024 alone, and issued a formal show-cause notice in October 2024 for violations of the Consumer Protection Act 2019.[2] These failures were not caused by a bad product in isolation — they were caused by a missing data and software management layer that left faults invisible until they became breakdowns.

The AI and connected layer described in this section is not a feature list. It is the operational infrastructure that makes a high-volume vehicle deployment in India manageable, safe, and compliant. The India connected vehicles market was valued at USD 7.5 billion in 2025 and is projected to reach USD 27.8 billion by 2034 at a 15.24% CAGR,[3] reflecting how quickly this infrastructure is becoming standard rather than premium. The India commercial telematics market specifically was USD 1.97 billion in 2025, growing at 15.37% CAGR to USD 7.14 billion by 2034.[4] The platform proposed here must be built to that standard from day one.

6a. Telematics & Vehicle Data

What Is Collected and at What Resolution

The foundation of every other capability described in this section is continuous, high-resolution telemetry from each vehicle. A validated real-world fleet study conducted on 1,114 EVs by Betterwhy Inc. (February 2025–2026) established the working data standard: pack voltage, current, state of charge (SOC), temperature, system accumulated capacity, individual cell voltages, mileage, and BMS state flags — collected at approximately one-second resolution and stored in a time-series database (TimescaleDB with hypertable partitioning for efficient range queries).[5] Cell-level voltage rather than pack-average is the critical distinction. Pack-level data can mask an individual cell failure until the entire pack is compromised; cell-level visibility provides the early signal AI models need.

Minimum Telemetry Data Classes — This Vehicle
  • Battery / power: Individual cell voltages and temperatures; pack current; State of Charge (SOC); State of Health (SoH) as a degradation curve, not binary flag; charge-discharge C-rate per cycle; BMS fault flags. For CNG variants: fuel-line pressure, injector duty cycle, tank temperature.
  • Powertrain / motion: Motor phase current; controller temperature; torque demand vs. actual output; three-axis IMU (accelerometer + gyroscope) at ≥50 Hz; wheel-speed per axle.
  • Positioning & connectivity: GPS positional fix and accuracy metric (HDOP); GPS antenna hardware revision (hardware-firmware mismatch detection); network uptime and packet-loss rate per vehicle.
  • Software state: Firmware version per ECU/module; hardware revision per component; active and historical diagnostic trouble codes (DTCs); OTA partition slot status (A/B) and last update timestamp.
  • Usage patterns: km/day; trips/day; idle time; charging session duration and energy consumed; duty-cycle intensity classification (urban stop-go vs. highway).

Transmission Architecture

Data moves from on-board sensors to the CAN bus, through the Telematics Control Unit (TCU), and to the cloud over a cellular connection. For IoT vehicle telemetry, MQTT is the standard protocol for low-bandwidth environments because it was specifically designed for constrained networks with unreliable connectivity — a direct requirement for Indian rural and peri-urban routes where 4G coverage is intermittent. The TCU must include a local data buffer sufficient to hold a minimum of 72 hours of telemetry in offline conditions, with a defined sync priority queue (safety faults first, then health data, then usage logs) when connectivity restores. This is a non-negotiable design requirement for Indian operating conditions. Fleet management software and AI analytics platforms are the fastest-growing segment in India's telematics market,[4] precisely because operators who have tried cloud-only solutions have run into this problem.

6b. Fleet & Battery Management

OTA Software Update Management — Why This Is a Safety Variable, Not a Feature

Software updates to vehicle ECUs — covering powertrain control, charging management, braking support, and energy management — are no longer a marketing differentiator. Once OTA updates touch those systems, "release cadence stops being a marketing metric. It becomes an output of system safety, configuration control, regulatory evidence, and fleet observability."[6] The two governing standards are UN Regulation No. 156, which formalizes the concept of a Software Update Management System (SUMS) and mandates that every update be delivered safely, securely, and traceably,[7] and ISO 24089:2023, which governs software update engineering at both organizational and vehicle-project levels.[8] Both are now enforceable for new vehicle types in 54 signatory countries after July 2024.[9] Type approval depends on SUMS certification. There is no compliant path that avoids this.

The operational mechanism mandated by UN R156 is a staged rollout: updates begin with a small, low-risk cohort of the fleet, telemetry is observed, and expansion proceeds only if defined gates are cleared. The release-control system must monitor "installation failures, diagnostic trouble codes, resets, battery drain, connectivity loss, functional regressions, and changes in customer complaints, distinguishing an update-caused anomaly from the fleet's normal background noise quickly enough to stop expansion."[6] The OEM maintains a hardware-software compatibility database; before targeting any vehicle ID, the campaign system queries this database and automatically excludes incompatible vehicles.[10] This is the direct fix for the GPS antenna-firmware mismatch class of failures.

A/B Dual-Partition Architecture New firmware downloads into a standby secondary partition (Slot B) while the primary partition (Slot A) runs verified, stable software. On completion, the system attempts to boot from Slot B. If verification fails — due to corruption, compatibility errors, or any post-install fault — the system automatically reverts to Slot A without manual intervention and without vehicle downtime.[11] This is the direct technical fix for the "scooter won't turn on after update" failure class. The watchdog-triggered fallback operates at hardware level, not software level, meaning a partially-written or corrupted update cannot leave the vehicle inoperable.[12]

The risk-scoring model that determines cohort assignment must be built on measurable vehicle-level features: hardware revision per component, sensor calibration age, prior fault history count, and connectivity uptime percentage. Vehicles scoring highest on instability across these features are excluded from early cohorts regardless of how well the update performed in pre-release testing. Deployment is precisely where risk materializes that testing did not reveal, because hardware variants, uneven connectivity, and real-world edge cases only appear at scale.

Battery State-of-Health and Remaining Useful Life

Battery health monitoring is the single highest-value AI function for a commercial EV fleet. The global EV battery health monitoring market was valued at USD 9.1 billion in 2024.[13] In January 2025, BatteryOK Technologies deployed its AI-enabled EV Doctor platform to over 1,500 EV service centers in India, able to assess battery health across any vehicle make or model in under 15 minutes.[13] This is the direction the Indian EV service market is moving.

The technical approach for this vehicle's battery management system uses AI-driven methods to predict State of Health (SoH) and Remaining Useful Life (RUL). Deep learning models — specifically LSTM and Bidirectional LSTM architectures — analyze signals including current, voltage, impedance, and capacity measured across charge-discharge cycles to model the degradation curve over time, rather than relying on static formula-based approaches.[14] The key distinction for fleet operators is that RUL prediction provides a timeline to failure, not a binary health status. A battery flagged at 75% SoH with a modeled RUL of 60 days is an actionable scheduling signal; a binary "good / bad" flag is not. Toyota Motor Engineering is currently deploying the RUL-QMoE framework — a probabilistic mixture-of-experts model — specifically for EV predictive maintenance, described as powering "Battery AI agents" for this purpose.[15]

For this proposal, the SoH threshold for fleet-operator action is set at 75–80% of rated capacity, at which point the battery should be scheduled for assessment and potential swap during planned downtime — not in response to a mid-route range failure. India will generate approximately 50,000 tonnes of spent EV batteries annually by 2030,[16] making second-life eligibility tracking (batteries above a minimum SoH threshold being flagged for stationary storage rather than disposal) both an economic and an environmental output of this system.

Fleet Operator Dashboard

The fleet-operator interface is not a consumer app. It is a multi-vehicle operational tool built for someone managing hundreds of vehicles across a city. The key views are: OTA campaign status per vehicle and per cohort; active fault summary ranked by severity; predicted downtime calendar (which vehicles, when, what fault class); battery SoH distribution across the fleet; and connectivity health per vehicle. Tata Motors had over 600,000 commercial vehicles connected through its Fleet Edge platform as of 2025,[17] demonstrating that enterprise-scale fleet connectivity is already operational in India. The benchmark exists. Fleet management software and AI analytics platforms represent the fastest-growing segment of India's commercial telematics market,[4] driven specifically by operators moving from hardware GPS tracking to cloud-delivered intelligence. This platform must sit in that category, not below it.

6c. Driver & Rider Apps

Driver-Facing Fault Interface

Raw diagnostic trouble codes (DTCs) are not a driver interface. A driver stopped mid-route by a frozen display and an unrecognized error code cannot make a safe decision — this is precisely the failure mode documented in Ola's service complaints. The on-board fault interface must translate any active DTC into one of three plain-language states: safe to continue, reboot the vehicle, or book service now. This translation can happen at the cloud level (NLP model mapping DTC taxonomy to severity tier) or on-edge if connectivity is unavailable — the latter is required for Indian operating conditions. The driver instruction must also function if the primary display is frozen: audio alert and, where the vehicle supports it, haptic feedback through the handlebars. This is not a design preference; it is the minimum viable fault communication for a vehicle operating in Indian urban traffic.

Data Transparency and DPDP Act Compliance

India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 (DPDP Act) was notified with its accompanying DPDP Rules on November 13, 2025. Full substantive compliance obligations take effect from May 13, 2027 — 18 months post-notification — but the compliance architecture must be designed in now, not retrofitted.[18] The DPDP Act is unambiguous about telematics data: "when linked to an identifiable individual, such data constitutes personal data under the DPDP Act."[19] This covers GPS location, route history, driving behavior, income patterns derived from trip frequency, and battery usage data where it can be tied to a driver identity.

The specific obligations for fleet operators under this framework are: consent must be "free, specific, informed and unambiguous" before any personal data processing;[20] "always-on" tracking must be clearly justified and limited to legitimate operational needs;[21] cross-border data transfers are permitted only to government-notified jurisdictions;[22] and large-scale platforms may be classified as Significant Data Fiduciaries, carrying heightened obligations.[19] Grant Thornton's automotive and EV industry analysis is explicit: "The DPDPA 2025 rules are set to redefine how the automotive and EV ecosystem approaches data... demanding privacy-by-design in telematics, predictive maintenance, and mobility services."[23]

The practical design implications for this vehicle's driver app: a clear disclosure screen at onboarding showing exactly what data is collected, at what frequency, who sees it, and the retention period; a driver-visible log of their own data footprint; no sharing of route history, income-pattern data, or GPS traces with third parties without separate specific consent; and no remote vehicle disable function operable without confirmed driver notification. The last point is particularly important for the e-rickshaw and last-mile delivery driver segment, where BMS remote-disable capability was identified as a primary adoption barrier — drivers discovering that a BMS app could cut vehicle power mid-route with no warning destroyed trust in the entire EV category for that segment.

Proactive Notifications

The driver app's most valuable function is not monitoring — it is anticipation. Predictive maintenance outputs (described in the next section) generate a maintenance forecast with a timeline. That forecast must be delivered to the driver as a specific, actionable notification timed to their normal off-shift period, not mid-route. The notification should name the likely issue, the estimated time window before it becomes a problem, and a pre-populated service booking option. This is the mechanism by which a "12-day failure forecast" becomes a "planned 45-minute service visit during the driver's morning off-window" rather than an unplanned breakdown.

6d. Safety & Predictive Maintenance

CNN/LSTM Fault Classification

The fault detection architecture for this vehicle is built on a hybrid CNN/LSTM deep-learning model trained on vibration, phase current, and temperature signals. The technical basis for this choice is well-established in the peer-reviewed literature. A 2024 study on New Energy Vehicle fault diagnosis using a CNN-LSTM integrated model reported 97.2% overall accuracy across ten common fault types, an F1-score of 0.968, and a real-time diagnosis delay of only 15ms. Critically, the same model maintained 93.7% accuracy on new vehicle data outside the training set, confirming generalization beyond the training fleet.[24] A broader review of CNNs applied to motor fault diagnosis and inverter fault detection confirmed that classification accuracy "is often above 97%," with CNNs showing "better classification accuracy than traditional ML techniques" specifically for these signal types.[25]

The architecture matters: CNNs extract spatial and frequency features from raw vibration and current waveforms (including FFT spectra and time-frequency representations), while LSTMs model the temporal progression of degradation — the sequence of how a fault develops over time, not just whether one exists at a given instant. This combination is specifically suited to the failure modes relevant to this vehicle: bearing wear, winding insulation degradation, IGBT faults in the inverter, and hub motor current anomalies. A 2026 study on HEV power system fault diagnosis using CNN-LSTM reported superior diagnostic performance for battery degradation, inverter anomalies, and motor failures compared to standalone CNN or LSTM models.[26]

97.2% accuracy, 15ms latency CNN-LSTM integrated model for New Energy Vehicle fault diagnosis across ten fault types, maintaining 93.7% on out-of-sample vehicle data. F1-score: 0.968. Published: IOP Publishing, 2024.[24]

Failure Timeline Prediction

Fault classification tells you a fault exists or is developing. Failure timeline prediction tells you when it will cross into operational failure — and that is the number fleet operators can actually act on. The AI model outputs a days-to-failure estimate rather than a binary fault/no-fault flag, using per-vehicle usage history to fine-tune the global model to each vehicle's actual duty cycle. The practical result is that a maintenance intervention can be scheduled during a driver's normal downtime — not in response to a roadside breakdown.

The validation data for this approach: a 2026 field validation pipeline tested across a heterogeneous fleet of real vehicles in multiple countries including India achieved 100% detection of wear-driven failures, with failure prediction accuracy improving from a ±25-day margin of error to ±12.2 days after per-vehicle fine-tuning. This means a fault is flagged and schedulable approximately 12 days before it becomes a breakdown — sufficient time for planned workshop attendance and parts pre-positioning.[27]

Digital Twin Validation

Every OTA firmware update and every sensor anomaly flagged by the classifier is validated against a digital twin of the vehicle's motor current, thermal behavior, and vibration profile before any action is taken on the physical vehicle. The digital twin is a physics-informed simulation that replicates the expected behavior of the vehicle's components under normal operating conditions. Deviation from expected behavior — whether triggered by an incoming firmware update or a live sensor reading — produces a pass/fail signal.

This is the specific mechanism that would have caught Ola's GPS antenna-firmware mismatch before it reached riders. The mismatch was only discovered after deployment when a later OS update exposed it; a digital twin pre-deployment check would have caught it in the validation stage. Since 2024, research on digital twins for electric vehicle components has moved from conceptual studies to edge-based pilot systems capable of real-time monitoring and cloud-level analytics.[28] A validated vehicle network digital twin study reported 93.1% accuracy in replicating real-world average vehicle speeds and 97.1% accuracy in average trip length estimation.[29] An AI-IoT-DT integration study for battery lifecycle management reported 95% anomaly detection precision and 76% detection rate in field conditions.[30]

6e. The AI/Data Layer — What Connects Everything

The Risk-Scoring Engine

Every data stream described above feeds into a central risk-scoring engine that produces a single ranked score (0–100) per vehicle. The feature vector for this model includes: hardware revision per ECU (specifically the GPS antenna and hub motor controller hardware variants), sensor calibration age in days, cumulative fault-code count in the trailing 30-day window, and connectivity uptime percentage over the same period. This score determines which cohort a vehicle is assigned to for OTA updates, whether a vehicle should be grounded ahead of a predicted failure, and which vehicles the fleet-operator dashboard surfaces as highest-priority. The risk-scoring engine is the mechanism by which the platform avoids the Ola failure mode of treating all vehicles identically — a production fleet is not uniform, and update risk "needs to be deployed to the lowest-risk cohort first" precisely because hardware variants and real-world edge cases only appear at scale.[6]

DTC-to-Plain-Language Translation

Diagnostic Trouble Codes are a machine protocol, not a human interface. The AI layer must include a mapping model that translates any active DTC on this vehicle — across the full taxonomy of motor, battery, GPS, controller, and connectivity fault codes — into one of the three driver-facing states. This mapping can be rule-based for common codes and ML-assisted for edge cases. The model must be deployable on-edge (stored locally on the TCU) so that fault communication does not depend on connectivity. Minimum language support for the Indian market is Hindi and the regional language of primary deployment city.

Data Governance Layer

The data governance layer is not a legal formality — under the DPDP Act it is an operational requirement that carries regulatory risk if missing. The requirements, as established by the Act and confirmed by legal analysis specific to the automotive-telematics context:[22],[23]

DPDP Act Compliance Requirements — This Platform
  • Consent architecture: Separate, specific, informed consent required for each processing purpose. Operational telemetry (safety, maintenance) can be justified under legitimate operational need; behavioral profiling, income-pattern analysis, and sharing with third-party commercial partners require explicit opt-in consent.
  • Retention limits: Retention periods must be defined per data class. "Always-on" tracking without justification or without defined limits is non-compliant.
  • Data minimization: Only data necessary for the stated purpose may be collected. The platform should not collect data it does not use.
  • Cross-border transfers: Permitted only to government-notified jurisdictions. All primary data storage must be India-resident or in a notified country.
  • Breach notification: Mandatory notification to the Data Protection Board and affected individuals in the event of a breach, even where no financial loss occurs.
  • Driver rights: Right to access their own data; right to withdraw consent; right to grievance redressal. The driver app must surface these controls.

Substantive compliance obligations take effect May 13, 2027. A platform launching before that date still needs to be designed to these standards, because retrofitting data governance architecture after deployment is the most expensive way to achieve compliance — and the most likely to leave exploitable gaps.

Model Performance Monitoring

Accuracy benchmarks — 97.2% fault classification, ±12-day failure prediction — are training-time metrics. In production, model performance degrades as the fleet ages, as vehicle usage patterns shift, and as new hardware variants enter the fleet. The AI layer must include a production monitoring pipeline that tracks false-positive rates, missed-fault rates, and prediction-to-actual-failure accuracy on a rolling basis. A drift detection trigger (defined as false-positive rate rising above a pre-set threshold) initiates a retraining cycle on the accumulated field data. Per-vehicle fine-tuned models update on a defined cadence rather than continuously, to prevent unstable model behavior from individual vehicle outliers propagating fleet-wide. This is the difference between an AI system that was accurate at launch and an AI system that remains accurate as the fleet grows.
